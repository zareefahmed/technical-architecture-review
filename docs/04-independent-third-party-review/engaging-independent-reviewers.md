# Engaging Independent Reviewers

The previous chapters make the case for independent review. This one is the practical guide to actually getting it: finding reviewers, verifying that they really are independent, scoping the engagement, and working with them so the review changes outcomes instead of gathering dust on a shelf.

## Where independent reviewers come from

- **Independent consultancies and boutique architecture firms** whose business is advisory fees, not product resale or large-scale delivery. The business model matters more than the size. A firm that earns nothing from your technology choices can afford to be right.
- **Individual freelance architects** with broad experience across ecosystems. Often the best value for a focused decision review. Do check the breadth, though. A career spent inside one ecosystem imports familiarity bias, freelance or not.
- **Peer organizations.** Architects from a non-competing company in your sector, reviewing each other's work reciprocally. Cheap and contextually sharp, though politeness tends to soften the findings.
- **Academic and standards-community experts**, for deep technology questions, like what a database engine's consistency guarantees really are, or where a protocol's limits sit.

Be equally clear about who is not independent, whatever their branding says: the large consultancy that is also a certified delivery partner of your leading vendor candidate; the reseller's "advisory" arm; the vendor's own professional services division. The test is never the word "independent" in the marketing. It is the answer to this question: what does your firm earn, from whom, under each possible conclusion of this review?

## Verifying independence

Before the engagement starts, get these in writing:

1. **Disclosure of interests.** Vendor partnerships, referral arrangements, reseller agreements, certifications held, and any current or pipeline business with the vendors under review.
2. **No contingent upside.** The reviewer, and their firm, is ineligible for implementation work that flows from this review's recommendations. This is the single most important term. "Recommend a big build, then win the build" is the consultancy version of the sales-agent pattern.
3. **A fee that does not depend on the conclusions.** Fixed or time-based, owed regardless of what the review finds.
4. **A direct reporting line.** Findings go to the sponsor unfiltered. Not through the delivery team, and never through a vendor, for "review" first.

## Scoping the engagement

A good scope names the decisions, not just the documents:

- **The questions.** "Is the selection of platform X sound, against our requirements and against real alternatives?" beats "review our architecture."
- **The criteria.** The quality attributes and business constraints the review judges against, agreed with the sponsor.
- **The access.** Documents, decision records, contracts and pricing (not negotiable, because lock-in cannot be assessed without them), cost data, and interview time with architects, engineers, operators, and business owners.
- **The depth.** A pre-signature decision review can take one to two weeks. A full estate review can take one to three months. Match it to the [stage of the process](../03-architecture-review/review-process-and-lifecycle.md).
- **The output.** The [report structure](../05-templates-and-checklists/review-report-template.md), a findings playback with the team, and a briefing for the sponsor.

## Working with the reviewer

- **Time it before signatures.** The highest-value engagement is the [decision review](../03-architecture-review/review-process-and-lifecycle.md), while the terms can still change. A review commissioned after the contract is signed can only annotate the consequences.
- **Give real access, and expect verification.** A reviewer who only reads slides can only review slides. Expect them to sample code, configuration, and cost reports. That is what you are paying for.
- **Help the team engage instead of defending.** Brief the delivery team that findings are about decisions, not about people or performance. The playback session corrects facts. It does not negotiate conclusions.
- **Demand ranked, evidenced, actionable findings.** Every finding comes with evidence, impact, and a concrete recommendation (see [what to insist on](../03-architecture-review/benefits-and-outcomes.md)).
- **Close the loop.** An owner and a date for every accepted finding, and a short follow-up check on the serious items. Budget for that follow-up when you commission the review, or it will not happen.

## Handling the findings you did not want

Sooner or later an independent review will contradict an announced direction, a signed relationship, or a sponsor's stated preference. That possibility is exactly what the review was for. How the organization responds is the real test of the whole apparatus. If findings get suppressed, or reviewers quietly stop being rehired for being right, every future reviewer learns what is actually wanted, and the organization returns, by a longer road, to hearing only what it pays to hear. Sponsors should decide in advance that unwelcome findings go to the same decision forum as welcome ones.
